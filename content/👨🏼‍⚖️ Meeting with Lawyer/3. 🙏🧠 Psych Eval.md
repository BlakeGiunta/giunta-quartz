   - There was never any reason to begin with.
   - Indigent (filed Inability to pay court costs) and can't afford to do a third evaluation.
   - Ms Young's arguments against telehealth are baseless and pseudoscientific.
     - If an evaluation was conducted via telehealth, it remains fully valid under Texas law. The Texas Behavioral Health Executive Council and professional bodies like the American Psychological Association (APA) have long endorsed remote evaluations. Under **Texas Occupations Code § 111.001**, telehealth for mental health services is regulated and recognized. Remote evaluations possess the same scientific validity and admissibility in family court as in-person evaluations, provided standard clinical protocols are maintained.
   - There is no "good cause" (nor it is in contest).
     - In Texas, a family court cannot compel a party to undergo a psychological evaluation based simply on a general desire to probe the other side’s mental state. The requesting party (the movant) must formally demonstrate "good cause," and the mental condition must genuinely be "in controversy." If a court orders an exam without the required good-cause showing, it is considered an abuse of discretion and can be vacated via mandamus.
     - Texas appellate courts enforce a rigorous three-part test to determine if the "good cause" threshold in Rule 204.1 has been met. The party requesting the evaluation must demonstrate all three:
       1. **Relevance and Likely Benefit:** The proposed examination must be directly relevant to the core issues in the case (e.g., tying a parent's mental health explicitly to the child's best interests) and must be likely to produce relevant evidence.
       2. **Nexus to the Controversy:** There must be a reasonable connection between the condition to be examined and the legal dispute. The Texas Supreme Court has established that "routine allegations of mental anguish or emotional distress"—which are standard in emotional divorce proceedings—do not inherently put a party's mental condition in controversy. There must be specific allegations or evidence indicating a mental health issue that could directly impact the case.
       3. **No Less Intrusive Means:** Because mental evaluations invade a person's privacy, the movant must prove that the desired information cannot be obtained through less invasive discovery methods, such as depositions, interrogatories, or reviewing existing medical records.
